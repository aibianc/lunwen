# A Study on the Empowerment of Generative AI in Senior High School English Teaching

> **Working draft (Version 0.1)**  
> This is a preliminary literature-based draft for undergraduate thesis development. It does not claim to report original fieldwork or experimental results. Claims about actual classroom effects should be checked against the cited full texts and, where required by the supervisor, supported by school-based data.

## Abstract

The rapid development of generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) has created new possibilities for English teaching and learning. In senior high school English education, GenAI tools may support teachers in preparing instructional materials, designing differentiated activities, providing language feedback, and creating opportunities for interactive learning. Meanwhile, their use also raises concerns about students’ over-reliance, inaccurate or biased outputs, insufficient pedagogical integration, data privacy, and academic integrity. Drawing on relevant studies from China and other countries, this paper reviews the potential roles, practical challenges, and improvement strategies of GenAI in senior high school English teaching. It first clarifies the concept and main application modes of GenAI and discusses constructivist learning theory, human–AI collaboration, and personalized learning as theoretical perspectives. It then analyzes the possible contributions of GenAI to teaching-resource development, learning-channel expansion, and differentiated instruction. Finally, it proposes strategies that emphasize students’ learning agency, teacher-led instructional design, task-based classroom integration, differentiated support, and responsible-use guidelines. The paper argues that GenAI should function as an assistant rather than a replacement for teachers or students’ independent thinking. Its educational value depends not simply on access to technology, but on the alignment of AI use with curriculum objectives, learners’ developmental needs, and sound pedagogical principles.

**Keywords:** generative artificial intelligence; senior high school English teaching; human–AI collaboration; personalized learning; English language education

## Introduction

The development of digital technology has continuously influenced foreign-language education. In recent years, generative artificial intelligence (GenAI), particularly conversational systems based on large language models, has attracted considerable attention because it can generate and revise text, explain language points, answer questions, and support communication. These capabilities have created new possibilities for English teaching, including lesson preparation, material development, writing feedback, vocabulary learning, and simulated conversation (Kohnke et al., 2023; Kasneci et al., 2023).

For senior high school students in China, English learning involves more than memorizing vocabulary and grammatical rules. It also requires the development of language competence, cultural awareness, thinking quality, and learning ability. In actual classrooms, teachers may face constraints such as limited lesson time, diverse student proficiency levels, heavy workloads, and difficulty providing timely individualized feedback. GenAI may offer supplementary support by generating materials at different difficulty levels, explaining language expressions, and providing students with additional opportunities to practice. A Chinese empirical study on secondary-school English writing reported that GenAI feedback was associated with improvements in several dimensions of writing among students whose overall writing foundation was at an average level, although gains were more limited for some students with stronger writing foundations (Liu et al., 2025).

Nevertheless, the introduction of GenAI does not automatically improve teaching quality. AI-generated information may be inaccurate, incomplete, biased, or unsuitable for a particular teaching context. Students may accept generated answers without verification, use AI to complete tasks instead of learning through them, or disclose personal information when using online systems. UNESCO (2023) therefore emphasizes a human-centred approach, including attention to data privacy, age appropriateness, human agency, and the pedagogical suitability of AI systems. Research reviews also identify over-reliance, output quality, and academic integrity as important concerns (Albadarin et al., 2024; Lee et al., 2026).

Against this background, this paper examines how GenAI can support senior high school English teaching and how its risks can be addressed. It focuses on three questions: (1) What roles can GenAI play in senior high school English teaching? (2) What problems may arise when GenAI is introduced into teaching and learning? (3) What strategies can help teachers and students use GenAI appropriately and effectively? The paper adopts a literature-based approach, drawing on Chinese and international publications and policy guidance. It does not present original experimental findings; rather, it synthesizes existing research and develops practical suggestions for classroom use.

## 1. Literature Review

### 1.1 Previous Studies on the Empowerment of Generative AI in Language Teaching Abroad

International research on GenAI and language education has developed rapidly since the public release of ChatGPT. Kohnke, Moorhouse, and Zou (2023) discuss the affordances and limitations of ChatGPT in language teaching and learning. They identify possible applications in language practice, feedback, and resource development, while emphasizing that teachers and learners need the digital competencies to use the technology ethically and effectively. Their work provides a useful starting point for understanding GenAI as a language-learning support tool rather than an autonomous teacher.

Kasneci et al. (2023) examine the opportunities and challenges of large language models in education more broadly. They suggest that such models may support the creation of educational content, interaction, and personalized learning. At the same time, they draw attention to unreliable outputs, bias, misuse, and the need for human oversight and critical evaluation. Although their discussion covers education in general rather than senior high school English specifically, it offers a framework for analyzing both the instructional potential and the limitations of GenAI.

Systematic reviews provide a broader picture of the emerging evidence. Albadarin et al. (2024) reviewed empirical studies of ChatGPT in educational settings and found that learners had used it for instant feedback, explanations, writing support, summarization, translation, and personalized learning assistance. The review also noted evidence suggesting that overuse could negatively affect innovative capacities and collaborative learning in some contexts. A systematic review of ChatGPT and language education published in *Computers and Education: Artificial Intelligence* examined 36 publications from the first year of research and reported that much of the empirical work focused on higher education and ethical issues (A systematic review of the first year of publications on ChatGPT and language education, 2024). More recent classroom-focused synthesis by Lee et al. (2026) reviewed 49 empirical studies published between January 2023 and December 2024. It found that higher education was the dominant research setting, English was the main target language, and common GenAI roles included feedback provider, learning tutor, cognitive stimulator, interaction facilitator, and conversation partner. The review also identified a need for more research in K–12 contexts and for longer-term studies.

These studies indicate that international scholarship has begun to move from general discussion of AI possibilities toward empirical investigation of language-learning processes. However, the evidence base remains uneven. Many studies concern university learners, rely on self-reported perceptions, or focus on writing rather than the full range of senior high school English teaching. Therefore, findings from higher education should not be transferred directly to Chinese senior high school classrooms without considering students’ age, curriculum requirements, language proficiency, and school context.

### 1.2 Previous Studies on the Empowerment of Generative AI in Language Teaching at Home

In China, research on GenAI and foreign-language teaching has expanded alongside the development of educational digitalization. Early discussions often focused on the possible teaching scenarios and instructional strategies of ChatGPT and large language models. For example, Li Zuowen (2024) discusses scenarios and strategies for ChatGPT-enabled foreign-language teaching, while Jiao Jianli and Chen Ting (2023) analyze several scenarios in which large language models may support English teaching. These studies help explain how generative tools may be incorporated into language instruction, although their discussions are broader than senior high school English alone.

Research has also begun to examine specific language skills. Mao Yansheng, Wang Yihang, and Xing Yanru (2024) investigate ChatGPT-assisted feedback in senior high school English writing. Their study discusses feedback in vocabulary, grammar, and discourse, suggesting that AI-supported feedback may make writing feedback more detailed and interactive. Liu et al. (2025), in an empirical study published in *China Educational Technology*, compare AI feedback and teacher feedback in secondary-school English writing. Their reported findings indicate that GenAI feedback can support several dimensions of writing performance, while the effects may differ according to students’ initial writing proficiency. This is particularly relevant to the present study because it addresses secondary-school learners rather than university students.

Recent Chinese publications have also discussed GenAI in senior high school reading and writing instruction. For example, studies published in 2025 and 2026 have examined the value, challenges, and optimization paths of GenAI-supported high school English writing and reading. Such work commonly highlights resource development, personalized feedback, reading support, and classroom evaluation, while also warning against over-reliance, reduced learner agency, and inaccurate assessment.

Overall, domestic research has begun to provide more context-specific discussion and evidence for secondary-school English teaching. Nevertheless, several limitations remain. First, some publications are conceptual or strategy-oriented, and their recommendations may not yet be supported by large-scale or longitudinal classroom evidence. Second, existing empirical studies tend to focus on a particular skill, especially writing, while fewer studies examine the coordinated development of listening, speaking, reading, writing, and thinking quality. Third, there is a need to explore how teachers can integrate GenAI into curriculum objectives and classroom interaction without allowing technology to dominate the teaching process.

### 1.3 Summary of Previous Studies

The literature reviewed above reveals three major trends. First, researchers generally recognize that GenAI can support resource development, language practice, and feedback. Second, scholars increasingly emphasize risks such as inaccurate output, over-reliance, privacy concerns, and academic integrity. Third, although studies on GenAI and language learning are growing, research specifically addressing K–12 and senior high school contexts remains less developed than research on higher education (Lee et al., 2026).

This paper therefore focuses on senior high school English teaching and considers both the potential value and practical limitations of GenAI. It connects international discussions of responsible AI use with Chinese research on secondary-school English instruction, aiming to develop strategies that are pedagogically appropriate for the local context.

## 2. Related Concepts and Theoretical Basis

### 2.1 Overview of Generative Artificial Intelligence

#### 2.1.1 Definition of Generative Artificial Intelligence

Generative artificial intelligence refers to AI systems capable of producing new content, such as text, images, audio, video, and computer code, in response to instructions or input data. Large language models are an important category of GenAI. They are trained on large collections of text and generate language by predicting likely continuations based on the context provided. In educational settings, conversational systems based on these models can respond to questions, explain concepts, summarize texts, generate exercises, and provide suggestions for revising written work (Kasneci et al., 2023).

However, fluent language output should not be confused with guaranteed factual accuracy or genuine understanding. GenAI may produce plausible but incorrect information, invent references, or misunderstand the learning context. Consequently, its output should be treated as material for human evaluation rather than as unquestionable knowledge (UNESCO, 2023).

#### 2.1.2 Development History of Generative Artificial Intelligence

The development of GenAI is connected to advances in machine learning, deep learning, natural language processing, and large-scale neural networks. Earlier language-learning technologies mainly supported fixed exercises, machine translation, automated scoring, and rule-based dialogue. The development of large-scale pre-trained language models made it possible for AI systems to respond more flexibly to natural-language instructions and generate context-sensitive content.

The public release of ChatGPT in November 2022 brought conversational GenAI to widespread public attention and accelerated discussion of its educational applications. Since then, generative systems have increasingly incorporated multimodal capabilities and tools for processing different forms of information. This development has expanded the possible forms of classroom support, but it has also made questions of reliability, privacy, assessment, and educational governance more urgent (UNESCO, 2023).

#### 2.1.3 Main Application Modes of Generative Artificial Intelligence

In senior high school English teaching, GenAI may be used in several ways:

1. **Teaching-resource generation:** Teachers can ask AI systems to draft reading passages, dialogue examples, vocabulary exercises, grammar questions, writing prompts, and supplementary materials. Teachers must check language accuracy, difficulty, cultural appropriateness, and alignment with the curriculum before using them.
2. **Language explanation and learning support:** Students can request explanations of vocabulary, grammar, sentence structures, and textual meaning. AI can provide alternative explanations or examples, but learners should verify important information with textbooks and teachers.
3. **Writing assistance and feedback:** GenAI can identify possible grammatical errors, suggest vocabulary alternatives, comment on organization, and provide revision questions. It should support students’ revision process rather than write the entire assignment for them.
4. **Conversation and role-play:** AI can simulate everyday or academic communication scenarios, allowing students to practice asking and answering questions and to prepare for oral tasks.
5. **Differentiated practice:** Teachers can use AI to generate exercises at different levels of difficulty or provide additional scaffolding for students who need support.
6. **Teaching reflection and assessment support:** Teachers may use AI to draft rubrics, generate formative questions, or organize feedback. Final evaluation and instructional decisions should remain under teacher responsibility.

### 2.2 Theoretical Basis of This Study

#### 2.2.1 Constructivist Learning Theory

Constructivist perspectives emphasize that learners actively construct knowledge through interaction with tasks, prior knowledge, social contexts, and reflection. From this perspective, learning is not simply the passive reception of information. GenAI may provide prompts, examples, explanations, and feedback that help students explore language problems and revise their understanding. For example, when students encounter an unfamiliar expression in a reading passage, they may ask AI for possible meanings and examples, compare the explanation with the original context, and discuss the result with classmates.

However, if students simply copy AI-generated answers, the active construction of knowledge may be weakened. Therefore, GenAI-supported tasks should require students to explain their choices, compare alternatives, and revise their work. The teacher should design activities in which AI output becomes an object of analysis rather than a final answer.

#### 2.2.2 Human–AI Collaborative Teaching Theory

Human–AI collaboration refers to the coordinated use of AI capabilities and human judgment in completing tasks. In teaching, AI may efficiently generate examples, organize information, and provide immediate language suggestions, while teachers contribute professional knowledge, emotional support, classroom management, and an understanding of students’ individual circumstances. Kohnke et al. (2023) emphasize the competencies required for teachers and learners to use ChatGPT effectively in language education. UNESCO (2023) similarly advocates a human-centred approach that protects human agency.

In this study, human–AI collaboration means that teachers remain responsible for learning objectives, task design, content selection, and final evaluation. Students use AI as a supplementary resource, while remaining responsible for their own thinking, decisions, and learning outcomes.

#### 2.2.3 Personalized Teaching Theory

Personalized teaching recognizes that students differ in prior knowledge, language proficiency, learning pace, interests, and learning needs. A single teaching task may not provide sufficient support or challenge for every learner. GenAI may help teachers prepare texts at different levels, generate additional examples, and offer targeted practice. For instance, students who have difficulty with sentence structure may receive sentence-combination exercises, while more advanced students may be asked to compare rhetorical choices or evaluate the reliability of AI-generated interpretations.

Nevertheless, AI-generated personalization is not automatically accurate. Teachers need to review the materials and make decisions based on classroom observation and students’ actual performance. Personalization should support equitable learning opportunities rather than label or restrict students.

## 3. The Empowering Roles of Generative AI in Senior High School English Teaching

### 3.1 Enriching Teaching Resources

The first potential contribution of GenAI is its ability to assist teachers in developing and adapting teaching resources. Preparing suitable reading materials, dialogues, vocabulary exercises, and writing tasks can require considerable time. GenAI can provide initial drafts or alternative versions of materials according to a specified topic, language level, or teaching objective. Teachers can then select, revise, and supplement the generated content.

For example, when teaching a unit about environmental protection, a teacher may use AI to draft a short reading passage, create comprehension questions, and generate a dialogue between students discussing local environmental issues. The teacher can adapt the language and content to the textbook and students’ proficiency. This process may increase the variety of classroom materials and allow more time for instructional interaction. However, AI-generated materials should be checked for factual accuracy, cultural sensitivity, language naturalness, and consistency with curriculum objectives (Kasneci et al., 2023; UNESCO, 2023).

### 3.2 Expanding Learning Channels

GenAI can provide students with additional opportunities to practice English beyond the limited time available in class. Students may use conversational systems to ask questions about grammar, request example sentences, summarize reading passages, or rehearse dialogues. In writing activities, AI may provide suggestions about vocabulary, grammar, and organization, enabling students to review their drafts before submitting them.

Research on language teaching has identified feedback, tutoring, and conversation practice as potential roles of GenAI (Kohnke et al., 2023; Lee et al., 2026). Chinese studies on secondary-school writing also suggest that AI-assisted feedback may help students notice language problems and revise their work (Mao et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025). These findings support the possibility of extending learning opportunities, although they do not prove that every AI tool or every form of use will improve learning outcomes.

### 3.3 Facilitating Layered Teaching

Students in the same senior high school class may have different levels of vocabulary, grammar, reading comprehension, and writing ability. GenAI may help teachers prepare differentiated tasks. For students who need more support, AI can generate vocabulary lists, sentence frames, and guided questions. Students with intermediate proficiency may complete comprehension and paragraph-writing tasks. More advanced students may analyze argument structure, compare different interpretations, or evaluate the quality of AI-generated writing.

Such differentiated activities may help teachers provide more appropriate learning support. The empirical findings reported by Liu et al. (2025), which indicate different patterns of writing improvement among students with different initial proficiency levels, also suggest that the effects of AI feedback should be considered in relation to learner differences. Importantly, teachers should use their own assessment and classroom observations to determine task levels rather than relying solely on AI judgments.

## 4. Existing Problems of Generative AI Empowered Senior High School English Teaching

### 4.1 Students’ Over-reliance on AI

One major concern is that students may become overly dependent on AI-generated answers. If students ask AI to translate every sentence, complete writing assignments, or answer reading questions without attempting the tasks independently, they may lose opportunities to develop vocabulary knowledge, grammatical awareness, reading strategies, and independent expression. Albadarin et al. (2024) report that some reviewed studies found potential negative effects of overuse on learners’ innovative capacities and collaborative learning competencies.

Over-reliance may also make it difficult for teachers to identify students’ actual language abilities. A polished AI-assisted assignment may not accurately represent the student’s independent performance. Therefore, AI use should be designed to support learning processes, not replace them.

### 4.2 Shallow Integration with Classroom Teaching

Another problem is the superficial use of GenAI. Teachers or students may use AI simply because it is new, without connecting it to clear learning objectives. For example, generating a large number of exercises does not necessarily improve learning if the exercises do not address students’ difficulties. Similarly, asking students to chat freely with AI may not lead to meaningful language development if there is no task, feedback, reflection, or follow-up assessment.

Effective integration requires teachers to consider what students should learn, why AI is needed, and how the generated output will be used. As Kohnke et al. (2023) and Kasneci et al. (2023) suggest, the educational value of language models depends on teachers’ and learners’ ability to use them critically and purposefully.

### 4.3 Lack of Standardized AI Usage Guidelines and Evaluation Mechanisms

GenAI use raises questions about privacy, accuracy, authorship, and fairness. Students may enter personal information or school-related data into online tools without understanding how the information may be processed. They may also use AI-generated content without acknowledging assistance, making it difficult to distinguish independent work from machine-generated work. In addition, AI systems may provide different answers to similar questions, and their evaluations may not always match teachers’ criteria.

UNESCO (2023) emphasizes the importance of privacy protection, age-appropriate use, human agency, and institutional validation of GenAI systems. In school contexts, clear guidance is needed to specify which tasks may use AI, how students should disclose AI assistance, what information must not be entered into external systems, and how teachers should evaluate students’ independent learning.

## 5. Strategies for Improving Generative AI Empowered Senior High School English Teaching

### 5.1 Guiding Students to Use AI Properly and Avoid Over-reliance

Teachers should explain that GenAI is a learning assistant rather than a substitute for independent thinking. Students can be encouraged to follow a process of attempting a task independently, consulting AI when necessary, checking the response, and revising their work in their own words. For example, in writing assignments, students should first produce an initial draft. They may then ask AI to identify possible grammar or organization problems, but they should decide which suggestions to accept and explain their revisions.

Teachers can also ask students to keep brief records of their AI use, including the questions they asked, the suggestions they received, and the changes they made. This makes the learning process more transparent and encourages critical reflection. Such practices are consistent with the emphasis on critical evaluation and human oversight in educational research and UNESCO guidance (Kasneci et al., 2023; UNESCO, 2023).

### 5.2 Deepening the Integration of AI with English Classroom Teaching

Teachers should begin with curriculum objectives and learning difficulties rather than with the capabilities of a particular AI tool. GenAI can be integrated into pre-class preparation, classroom activities, and after-class learning.

Before class, teachers may use AI to draft supplementary materials and questions, then review and revise them. During class, students may compare an AI-generated interpretation with their own reading, identify errors in a generated paragraph, or use AI-supported role-play to practice a communication task. After class, students may receive supplementary explanations or use feedback to revise their writing. In each stage, teachers should provide clear instructions and opportunities for students to explain their reasoning.

A useful principle is to make AI output part of the learning task. Instead of asking students simply to accept an AI answer, teachers can ask: Is the answer accurate? Which evidence in the text supports it? What has the AI overlooked? How would you improve the response? Such activities can develop language ability and critical thinking together.

### 5.3 Carrying Out Layered Teaching with AI

Teachers may use GenAI to prepare learning materials at different levels, while ensuring that all students work toward shared curriculum objectives. For example, in a reading lesson, students who need more support may receive vocabulary explanations and guiding questions; students at an intermediate level may summarize paragraphs and identify the main idea; advanced students may evaluate the author’s argument and compare alternative interpretations.

In writing lessons, differentiated support may include sentence frames for students who struggle with basic expression, paragraph-organization prompts for intermediate learners, and style or argument analysis for advanced learners. Teachers should regularly review students’ work and adjust the level of support. AI should not make final judgments about students’ ability or determine fixed ability groups. This approach uses technology to increase the availability of support while preserving teachers’ professional responsibility.

### 5.4 Standardizing AI Use and Improving Multi-dimensional Evaluation

Schools and teachers should establish clear and age-appropriate rules for AI use. These rules may include the following: students should not enter sensitive personal information; AI-generated facts and references must be checked; students should disclose substantial AI assistance when required; and AI should not be used to submit work that is expected to demonstrate independent performance. Teachers should also explain the limits of AI-generated feedback.

Evaluation should consider both the final product and the learning process. In English writing, for example, teachers may assess the initial draft, revision process, final text, and the student’s explanation of revision decisions. In reading, assessment may include comprehension, evidence-based interpretation, and the ability to identify errors in AI-generated explanations. A multi-dimensional approach can reduce the risk of treating fluent AI-generated output as direct evidence of student learning.

At the school level, teachers should receive professional development on prompt design, output verification, privacy protection, and instructional integration. The school should select tools that are appropriate for students’ age and local requirements. These measures reflect UNESCO’s (2023) recommendation that GenAI systems be evaluated for ethical and pedagogical suitability before being adopted in education.

## Conclusion

GenAI offers potential support for senior high school English teaching in China. It can help teachers develop and adapt teaching resources, provide students with additional opportunities for language practice, and support differentiated learning. Existing studies on language education and secondary-school English writing suggest that AI-assisted feedback and interaction may be useful, although the effects vary according to learners, tasks, and instructional contexts (Kohnke et al., 2023; Mao et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025).

At the same time, GenAI may lead to over-reliance, superficial classroom integration, inaccurate information, privacy risks, and difficulties in evaluating students’ independent abilities. These challenges indicate that technology alone cannot guarantee educational improvement. Teachers need to set clear learning objectives, evaluate AI-generated materials, guide students’ use of AI, and maintain responsibility for instructional decisions and assessment.

In conclusion, the appropriate direction is not to replace traditional English teaching with AI, but to build a teacher-led and student-centred model of human–AI collaboration. Future research should include more classroom-based studies in Chinese senior high schools, examine different language skills, compare learners with different proficiency levels, and investigate the long-term effects of GenAI-supported learning. Such evidence will help clarify when, how, and for whom GenAI can make a meaningful contribution to English education.
