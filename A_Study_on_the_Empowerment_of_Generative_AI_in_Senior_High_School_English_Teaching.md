# A Study on the Empowerment of Generative AI in Senior High School English Teaching

> **Working draft v0.1 — literature-based preliminary manuscript.** This draft does not claim original classroom experiments, interviews, questionnaires, or statistical findings. Verify every source against its full text and adapt the manuscript to the university's requirements before submission.

## Abstract

The rapid development of generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) has created new possibilities for senior high school English teaching. By generating and adapting texts, explaining language points, simulating communicative situations, and providing preliminary feedback, GenAI may support teachers in resource development and lesson preparation while offering students additional opportunities for language practice and self-directed learning. However, its educational value is not automatic. Potential problems include inaccurate or biased outputs, students’ over-reliance on generated answers, weakened independent thinking, privacy and copyright risks, unequal access, and superficial integration with classroom objectives. Drawing on research on generative AI in language education, international guidance, and Chinese studies on secondary-school English teaching, this paper examines the empowering roles, existing problems, and improvement strategies of GenAI in senior high school English teaching. It argues that GenAI should function as an assistant within a teacher-led, learner-centred model rather than replace teachers or students’ cognitive work. The paper proposes responsible-use instruction, task-based classroom integration, differentiated learning support, and process-oriented assessment as practical strategies. As a preliminary literature-based study, it provides a conceptual framework for subsequent classroom observation and empirical investigation.

**Keywords:** generative artificial intelligence; senior high school English; English language teaching; human-AI collaboration; differentiated instruction

## Introduction

Artificial intelligence has increasingly influenced educational practices, and the public release of generative AI applications has accelerated discussion about how teachers and learners may use these technologies. Unlike conventional digital resources that mainly store or display prepared materials, generative AI systems can produce new content in response to natural-language prompts. In English education, this capability makes it possible to generate reading passages, create example sentences, explain vocabulary and grammar, simulate conversations, and provide preliminary feedback on students’ writing.

Senior high school English teaching in China has specific educational objectives. The *English Curriculum Standards for Senior High Schools (2017 Edition, 2020 Revision)* identifies language ability, cultural awareness, thinking quality, and learning ability as the four dimensions of English subject core competencies. It also emphasizes students’ active participation in English learning activities and the use of information technology to support teaching (Ministry of Education of the People’s Republic of China, 2020). Therefore, the value of GenAI should not be judged simply by whether it makes exercises faster or produces fluent texts. Its value should be considered in relation to these educational objectives.

International research has discussed both the opportunities and limitations of ChatGPT and other large language models in education. Kohnke, Moorhouse, and Zou (2023) examine the affordances and drawbacks of ChatGPT for language teaching and learning, emphasizing the digital competencies needed for effective and ethical use. Lo et al. (2024), in a systematic review of empirical studies in ESL/EFL education, map the applications and research issues associated with ChatGPT. In China, studies have begun to examine concrete applications in high school English reading, writing, and continuation writing. Wan and Qian (2024), for example, discuss a human-AI collaborative teaching scenario for senior high school English continuation writing, while Liu et al. (2025) report an empirical study of generative-AI feedback in secondary-school English writing.

Although the literature is developing rapidly, several issues deserve further examination. Some discussions focus on the general advantages of AI but do not sufficiently connect these advantages with the learning objectives and classroom realities of senior high school English. Practical applications also need to distinguish between AI as a resource-generation tool, a learning partner, and a feedback assistant. Finally, the risks of inaccurate outputs, over-reliance, and inappropriate data use require concrete teaching and assessment responses.

Accordingly, this paper addresses three questions: (1) What roles can GenAI play in senior high school English teaching? (2) What problems may arise when GenAI is introduced into teaching and learning? (3) What strategies can help teachers and students use GenAI appropriately and effectively? The paper adopts a literature-based analytical approach. It synthesizes relevant international and Chinese publications and interprets their implications for senior high school English teaching. It does not claim to provide original experimental evidence.

## 1. Literature Review

### 1.1 Previous Studies on the Empowerment of Generative AI in Language Teaching Abroad

International research on AI-supported language learning predates ChatGPT. Earlier studies explored chatbots as conversational partners, tools for language practice, and supplementary learning resources. Fryer et al. (2020), for example, review the development and future directions of bots for language learning. This work provides a background for understanding the transition from rule-based or task-specific chatbots to more flexible generative systems.

After ChatGPT became publicly available, research attention expanded to the potential uses of large language models in language education. Kohnke, Moorhouse, and Zou (2023) discuss how ChatGPT can support language teaching and learning, including the generation of teaching materials and language-learning support. They also identify limitations and stress the importance of digital competencies among teachers and learners. Their discussion suggests that the pedagogical value of a chatbot depends not only on its technical functions but also on how teachers design learning tasks and guide students’ use.

Lo et al. (2024) provide a systematic review of empirical studies concerning ChatGPT in ESL/EFL education. Their review is useful because it moves beyond isolated examples and organizes research on applications and related issues. The literature indicates that generative AI has been explored in areas such as writing assistance, language practice, feedback, and learner support. Nevertheless, findings from university or adult language-learning contexts cannot automatically be generalized to Chinese senior high school students. Differences in age, language proficiency, curriculum requirements, assessment pressure, and school policies need to be considered.

Research on large language models in education also identifies broader opportunities and risks. Kasneci et al. (2023) discuss the potential of large language models to support educational content creation, interaction, and personalized learning, while drawing attention to concerns such as inaccurate information and the need for critical evaluation. UNESCO (2023) similarly recommends a human-centred approach, emphasizing privacy, inclusion, age appropriateness, human agency, and institutional validation of educational uses.

Overall, international research provides a conceptual and practical foundation for studying GenAI in English teaching. However, more context-sensitive research is needed on how these tools can support the particular curriculum, assessment practices, and developmental needs of senior high school students in China.

### 1.2 Previous Studies on the Empowerment of Generative AI in Language Teaching at Home

In China, research on AI-assisted foreign-language teaching has developed from broader discussions of educational technology toward more specific classroom applications of generative AI. Recent studies have examined how ChatGPT can support reading, writing, vocabulary learning, and classroom interaction in secondary-school English.

Wan and Qian (2024) discuss the construction of a human-AI collaborative teaching scenario for senior high school English continuation writing. Their article explores the use of ChatGPT in text analysis, vocabulary explanation, summarization, and the generation and revision of continuation-writing examples. It also presents a prompt structure based on background, task, and conditions. This study offers a practical reference for integrating generative AI into a task particularly relevant to Chinese high school English teaching.

Liu et al. (2025) investigate the role of generative-AI feedback in secondary-school English writing through a comparison of experimental and control groups across multiple writing assessments. The study examines dimensions including relevance of content, grammatical accuracy, lexical and sentence-level accuracy, and logical expression. It is especially relevant because it moves beyond a general discussion of AI functions and considers student writing performance.

Other Chinese publications have discussed the use of generative AI in high school English reading and teaching-resource development. Wu (2025) examines the application of generative AI in senior high school English reading teaching, including resource integration, teaching-method innovation, and evaluation. Such work indicates that Chinese researchers and teachers are exploring how AI can be connected with specific teaching tasks rather than treated only as a general-purpose technology.

Despite these developments, several limitations remain. Some publications are primarily instructional reflections or teaching-strategy discussions, while rigorous longitudinal or controlled empirical studies remain comparatively limited. Research findings may also be tied to particular textbooks, classes, or teaching activities, making broad generalization difficult. Further research should examine not only immediate language scores but also students’ independent learning, critical thinking, information evaluation, and long-term language development.

### 1.3 Summary of Previous Studies

The literature reviewed above reveals three common themes. First, GenAI can support language learning by generating materials, simulating interaction, and providing feedback. Second, its use creates risks involving accuracy, dependence, privacy, and academic integrity. Third, effective application requires teacher guidance and a clear connection between technology and learning objectives.

However, a gap remains between general claims about GenAI and its systematic integration into Chinese senior high school English teaching. This paper therefore focuses on three dimensions of teaching empowerment—resource enrichment, learning-channel expansion, and differentiated instruction—and connects them with the problems and strategies discussed in later chapters.

## 2. Related Concepts and Theoretical Basis

### 2.1 Overview of Generative Artificial Intelligence

#### 2.1.1 Definition of Generative Artificial Intelligence

Generative artificial intelligence refers to AI systems capable of producing new content, such as text, images, audio, video, or computer code, in response to user instructions or other input. Large language models are an important type of generative AI. They are trained on large collections of text and generate language by predicting likely sequences of tokens based on context. As a result, they can perform tasks such as summarization, translation, explanation, dialogue, and text generation.

In education, GenAI should be understood as a content-generation and interaction technology rather than an authoritative source of knowledge. Its responses may contain factual errors, fabricated references, inappropriate examples, or cultural bias. UNESCO (2023) therefore argues for human-centred and age-appropriate educational use, with attention to data protection and the pedagogical suitability of tools.

#### 2.1.2 Development History of Generative Artificial Intelligence

The development of generative AI is connected with advances in machine learning, deep learning, natural language processing, and large-scale neural networks. Earlier educational chatbots generally relied on predefined rules or relatively limited dialogue functions. Later neural language models improved the ability to process context and generate coherent language. The Transformer architecture introduced by Vaswani et al. (2017) became an important foundation for subsequent large language models.

The public release of ChatGPT in November 2022 brought conversational generative AI into widespread public use. Since then, generative AI tools have developed rapidly in their language, multimodal, and interactive capabilities. In education, this development has prompted teachers, researchers, and policymakers to reconsider teaching resources, student assignments, assessment, and academic integrity (Miao & Holmes, 2023).

#### 2.1.3 Main Application Modes of Generative Artificial Intelligence

In senior high school English teaching, GenAI may be used in several ways.

First, it can generate and adapt learning materials. Teachers may ask it to produce reading passages, vocabulary exercises, example sentences, dialogue scripts, or questions at different difficulty levels. All generated materials should be checked for accuracy, appropriateness, and alignment with curriculum objectives.

Second, it can serve as a language-practice partner. Students may use a conversational system to rehearse dialogues, practice asking and answering questions, or explore expressions in simulated situations. Such interaction can provide additional practice opportunities, although it cannot fully replace communication with teachers and classmates.

Third, it can provide preliminary learning feedback. In writing activities, GenAI may identify possible grammatical problems, suggest alternative expressions, or ask questions that encourage students to revise their arguments. The final judgment should remain with the teacher and learner, because generated feedback may itself be inaccurate or unsuitable.

Fourth, it can support lesson preparation and differentiated instruction. Teachers may use it to brainstorm activities, create supplementary examples, simplify or enrich texts, and prepare exercises for students with different proficiency levels. Teachers must review the output before using it in class.

### 2.2 Theoretical Basis of This Study

#### 2.2.1 Constructivist Learning Theory

Constructivist perspectives understand learning as an active process in which learners construct and revise knowledge through interaction with tasks, contexts, and other people. From this perspective, GenAI may provide prompts, examples, explanations, and alternative viewpoints that help students explore language and meaning. For example, in a reading lesson, students can compare their own interpretation of a text with an AI-generated explanation and identify differences.

However, constructivist learning does not mean accepting generated answers passively. Students need to question, verify, discuss, and revise information. Teachers should design tasks that require learners to explain their choices and demonstrate their own understanding.

#### 2.2.2 Human-AI Collaborative Teaching

Human-AI collaboration refers to the coordinated use of AI capabilities and human professional judgment. In teaching, AI can assist with repetitive or preparatory tasks, generate alternative materials, and provide preliminary feedback. Teachers, however, remain responsible for setting learning objectives, understanding students’ needs, selecting materials, interpreting assessment evidence, and providing emotional and ethical guidance.

The human-AI collaborative scenario discussed by Wan and Qian (2024) illustrates how ChatGPT may be incorporated into senior high school continuation-writing instruction. The central principle is that AI supports the teaching process while teachers retain pedagogical leadership and students remain responsible for their own learning.

#### 2.2.3 Personalized and Differentiated Teaching

Students in the same class may differ in vocabulary size, reading speed, grammatical knowledge, confidence, and learning needs. Differentiated teaching seeks to provide appropriate support while maintaining shared educational objectives. GenAI may help teachers prepare texts at different levels of complexity, generate additional exercises, and offer alternative explanations.

Nevertheless, AI-generated personalization should not be confused with accurate diagnosis of a learner. Teachers need to use classroom observation, student work, and assessment evidence to decide what support is appropriate. AI recommendations should be treated as suggestions rather than final judgments.

## 3. The Empowering Roles of Generative AI in Senior High School English Teaching

### 3.1 Enriching Teaching Resources

A common challenge in English teaching is the need to select materials that are both relevant to the curriculum and suitable for students’ language levels. GenAI can help teachers develop supplementary reading passages, dialogues, vocabulary examples, writing prompts, and formative exercises. It can also adapt a text by changing its length, vocabulary difficulty, or sentence structure.

For example, when teaching a unit about environmental protection, a teacher may use GenAI to draft a short news-style passage, prepare comprehension questions, and generate a dialogue between students discussing local environmental actions. The teacher can then compare the generated content with the textbook and reliable sources, correct errors, and adapt the material to the class.

The main benefit is not that AI can replace textbooks or authentic materials. Rather, it can help teachers create supplementary resources more efficiently and provide more options for lesson design. This use is consistent with the broader educational possibilities discussed by Kasneci et al. (2023) and UNESCO (2023).

### 3.2 Expanding Learning Channels

Traditional classroom instruction provides limited time for every student to practice speaking, ask questions, and receive individual feedback. A conversational GenAI tool may offer additional opportunities for students to practice language outside class. Students can simulate situations such as ordering food, asking for directions, introducing a cultural tradition, or discussing a social issue.

GenAI can also explain vocabulary and grammar in different ways. When students do not understand an explanation in the textbook, they may request simpler examples or ask the system to contrast two expressions. In writing practice, they may ask for feedback on clarity or organization and then decide which suggestions to adopt.

These functions can support self-directed learning, but they should be integrated with classroom tasks. Teachers can ask students to keep a record of their prompts, compare AI feedback with their own revisions, and explain why they accepted or rejected particular suggestions. Such activities make the learning process visible and reduce the risk of passive copying (Kohnke et al., 2023; Lo et al., 2024).

### 3.3 Facilitating Differentiated Teaching

In a mixed-ability classroom, a single text or exercise may be too difficult for some students and insufficiently challenging for others. GenAI can help teachers prepare multiple versions of a reading passage, provide vocabulary support, or create extension questions. Students who need foundational support may receive a version with shorter sentences and glossed vocabulary, while more proficient students may work with a more complex text and questions requiring inference or evaluation.

In writing lessons, students may receive different forms of scaffolding. Some may need help organizing a paragraph, while others may benefit from questions about argument quality, coherence, and audience awareness. AI can generate possible scaffolds, but teachers should select them according to students’ demonstrated needs.

Differentiation should not label students permanently or lower expectations for particular groups. Instead, it should provide flexible support that helps all learners progress toward shared curriculum goals. This approach is compatible with the curriculum standards’ emphasis on meeting students’ different developmental needs (Ministry of Education, 2020).

## 4. Existing Problems of Generative AI Empowered Senior High School English Teaching

### 4.1 Students’ Over-reliance on AI

One major risk is that students may use GenAI to complete assignments without engaging in the underlying learning process. For example, a student may ask AI to write an entire essay, translate a passage, or answer reading questions and then submit the output as personal work. Such practices may reduce opportunities to develop vocabulary, grammar, reasoning, and independent expression.

Over-reliance can also make students less willing to tolerate uncertainty or solve language problems independently. If students accept fluent AI-generated answers without checking them, they may learn incorrect information or imitate expressions that are inappropriate for the task. Kohnke et al. (2023) and Kasneci et al. (2023) discuss the need to address the limitations and risks of generative systems.

### 4.2 Shallow Integration with Classroom Teaching

Another problem is the superficial use of GenAI. Teachers or students may use it simply to generate a worksheet, translate a text, or produce a model essay, without changing the structure of learning activities. In such cases, the technology is added to the classroom but does not necessarily improve student participation, language use, or higher-order thinking.

Effective integration requires a clear relationship between the AI activity and the lesson objective. If the objective is to develop critical reading, students should evaluate claims and evidence rather than merely ask AI to summarize a passage. If the objective is to improve writing, students should plan, draft, revise, and reflect instead of copying a completed AI-generated essay.

### 4.3 Lack of Standardized AI Usage Guidelines and Evaluation Mechanisms

The use of GenAI raises questions about acceptable assistance, authorship, data protection, copyright, and assessment. If schools do not explain what kinds of AI use are permitted, students may have different understandings of academic integrity. Teachers may also find it difficult to distinguish between legitimate language support and the substitution of AI-generated work for student effort.

Furthermore, conventional assessment may focus mainly on the final product. When a polished assignment can be generated with AI, the final text alone may not accurately represent a student’s language ability or learning process. UNESCO (2023) emphasizes the need for human-centred governance, data protection, and institutional consideration of the ethical and pedagogical suitability of GenAI tools.

## 5. Strategies for Improving Generative AI Empowered Senior High School English Teaching

### 5.1 Guiding Students to Use AI Properly and Avoid Over-reliance

Schools and teachers should provide explicit instruction on responsible AI use. Students need to understand that GenAI is a support tool, not an unquestionable authority or a substitute for their own learning. Teachers can demonstrate how to verify AI-generated facts, compare alternative answers, identify possible errors, and revise generated language.

A practical rule is to require students to complete an initial attempt before consulting AI. For example, students can first write their own paragraph, then request feedback on grammar or organization, and finally submit both the original and revised versions with a short explanation of the changes. This process helps preserve student agency and makes AI use transparent.

Teachers should also explain privacy and academic-integrity expectations. Students should avoid entering personal information, classmates’ identifiable data, or confidential school materials into public AI systems. The school should specify which tools are approved and what uses are allowed for different assignments, consistent with the human-centred principles recommended by UNESCO (2023).

### 5.2 Deepening the Integration of AI with English Classroom Teaching

Teachers should begin with curriculum objectives and then decide whether GenAI can meaningfully support a particular learning activity. AI should not be used merely because it is available. In a reading lesson, students may first read the original text independently, identify the main idea, and answer comprehension questions. They can then compare their interpretation with an AI-generated summary, locate omissions or inaccuracies, and discuss how textual evidence supports their conclusions.

In writing lessons, teachers can organize a process that includes brainstorming, outlining, drafting, AI-assisted feedback, peer review, and final revision. AI feedback should be one source of information rather than the final authority. Students should be required to justify revisions and retain responsibility for the final text.

This task-based approach connects technology use with language practice, critical thinking, and communication. It also reflects the broader principle that educational technology should serve learning objectives rather than determine them (Ministry of Education, 2020; UNESCO, 2023).

### 5.3 Carrying Out Differentiated Teaching with AI

Teachers can use GenAI to prepare flexible learning materials for students with different proficiency levels. For reading, this may include vocabulary glossaries, simplified explanations, and extension questions. For speaking, students may practice dialogues with different levels of complexity. For writing, teachers may provide optional scaffolds such as paragraph frames, linking expressions, or questions that help students develop arguments.

However, differentiated teaching should be based on teachers’ understanding of students rather than solely on AI-generated classifications. Teachers should review the materials for linguistic accuracy, cultural appropriateness, and alignment with the textbook. Students should also have opportunities to move between levels as their competence develops.

### 5.4 Standardizing AI Use and Improving Multi-dimensional Evaluation

Schools should develop clear and age-appropriate guidelines for AI use. These guidelines can define acceptable and unacceptable uses, explain how students should acknowledge AI assistance, and establish rules for protecting personal data and copyrighted materials. Teachers should receive training in prompt design, output verification, privacy protection, and the pedagogical limitations of generative systems.

Assessment should pay greater attention to the learning process. In addition to evaluating the final writing product, teachers can examine outlines, drafts, revision histories, peer feedback, oral explanations, and students’ reflections on AI suggestions. A student who can explain why a generated suggestion is inaccurate or unsuitable may demonstrate meaningful language awareness and critical thinking.

A multidimensional evaluation framework may include language accuracy, content relevance, coherence, communicative effectiveness, independent contribution, and responsible use of AI. The purpose is not to reward the amount of AI use, but to assess whether students have achieved the intended learning outcomes. Such a framework can help maintain academic integrity while recognizing the changing conditions of language learning.

## Conclusion

Generative AI offers new possibilities for senior high school English teaching by helping teachers enrich resources, expanding opportunities for language practice, and supporting differentiated learning. It may also assist with lesson preparation, writing feedback, and the design of communicative tasks. Nevertheless, these benefits depend on careful pedagogical design. Inaccurate outputs, student over-reliance, superficial classroom integration, privacy concerns, and weaknesses in assessment may undermine its educational value.

This paper argues that GenAI should be integrated through a teacher-led and learner-centred approach. Teachers should guide students to verify information, use AI transparently, and remain responsible for their own learning. Classroom activities should connect AI functions with curriculum objectives, while differentiated support and process-oriented assessment should help students develop language competence and critical thinking.

As a preliminary literature-based study, this paper has limitations. It does not include original classroom observations, interviews, questionnaires, or controlled experiments. In addition, some Chinese publications on this topic are recent and focus on particular teaching cases. Future research could investigate how different forms of AI support influence students’ English writing, reading comprehension, learning autonomy, and critical thinking over a longer period. Empirical studies involving multiple classes and schools would help provide stronger evidence for the effective and responsible use of GenAI in senior high school English teaching.
